High School AP Lit Essay on Toni Morrison's Beloved

Funny story about this essay, I only read about 15-20 pages of the book before writing this

Toni Morrison uses a fascinating blend of figurative language, evocative imagery, and complex narrative techniques to disarm and capture the reader in her postmodern masterpiece, Beloved. As her work unfolds, through her characters, through their choices, through the enduring physicality of it all, Morrison thrusts the reader into a unique world, where the human experience is explored outside of the context of the white man, outside of the grand story of good and evil, but instead in the singular, inside the bodies of those who have experienced Human, as symbols and as pillars of what life a human has lived, not just as vessels of their experience but also as records of their suffering.

Immediately, Morrison packs the weight of trauma into the body of the mother, Sethe. As Paul D appears at 124, as they begin to take off their clothes, the immediate sexual nature of their encounter is null compared to the deeper emotional weight that is on display. As Sethe removes her top, Paul inquires about the scars on her back, the mark from the cowhide. Sethe describes it as a “chokecherry tree,” complete with a “trunk, branches, and even leaves” (10). Morrison’s description of the tree, something growing on her back, is complex for a number of reasons. The figurative idea of something organic, something alive, growing on a person, shows the living that Sethe had experienced at Sweet Home, a living reminder on her body that she experienced the things that she has. A recurring theme in both this novel and Morrison’s other works, such as Sula, is the idea that a person cannot escape their past, cannot continue to repress it as Sethe tries ever so hard to do, but instead carries with them the weight of their life forever. Morrison posits the concept of rememory to explain this. In Morrison’s “rememory,” when something significant, or anything at all, happens at a certain place, that event will stay with the place. If a house burns down, “the picture of it… stays” (21). She proposes that even if everyone who knew that house died, if the last real memory of the house faded into oblivion, that rememory would still exist, would still be real, in Morrison’s world. In this passage about the tree on Sethe’s back, it is clear that it carries the “rememory” of her time at Sweet Home, how she was whipped, and how she is a living rememory of that time. Morrison takes the physical body even further, and even more tragic in Sethe’s focus, when asked about the scars. As Paul D inquires, Sethe consistently stresses not the whipping, not this torture that she was put through by her owners, but rather that they “took her milk” (12). Not that she was whipped, not that she was made an animal, but that they took her milk from her. A mother herself, Morrison writes heavily about the duties, responsibilities, and incomprehensible love a mother has for her children. A woman produces her own milk for her children, made by processes in her own body. For her milk, her own body, to be taken from her, that was worse than being an animal for her. That was worse than being whipped, being tortured, but rather her body being siphoned. Because for all the whipping that they were doing to her, they were adding to her experience, they were adding rememories to her body. Yet now, as they took away her milk, they were robbing her of her own body, robbing her of her soul, something that cannot be remembered, something that would be lost. Her motherhood was taken from her, and her soul with it.

The enduring physicality of the body as a symbol of the human experience is dandy, but keeping with the postmodern times, Morrison decides to ball out by contrasting it with metaphorical examinations of the inexplicably paraphysical. While all prior examples have been strictly physical, strictly abiding by the laws of the natural world, Morrison also uses symbols of grief and trauma that are abnormal, as can most clearly be pictured in the ghost of Sethe’s deceased infant daughter, Beloved. Beloved haunts 124 and can be interpreted to be a nonphysical manifestation of the grief that Sethe feels for liberating her child, conjoined with the grief the child has for being murdered by her own mother. In a passage from early on in the novel, Beloved smiles at Sethe and speaks to her, “Tell me your diamonds” (68). The ‘diamonds’ are a metaphor for the haunting trauma and past that Sethe has faced, and while previously Sethe is described by Morrison as having tried to think about her past as little as possible, here Sethe finds comfort in telling the stories of her past to Beloved. In her words, not her body, she liberates herself from the repression of her human suffering and immortalizes it in words, floating through the ether rather than in a physical vessel. The marked shift from the physical to the corporeal in her contrasting manifestations of suffering and experience demonstrates Morrison’s complex narrative technique, adding to the overwhelming world that the reader has been shoved into. In the same passage, however, the shadows of both Sethe and Beloved are projected onto the walls of the house, interacting with each other, dancing, fighting, as “their two shadows clashed and crossed on the ceiling like black swords” (68). This coexistence of both the physical and the paraphysical in one medium is used by Morrison to convey the sameness of the physical and the paraphysical manifestations of humanity. Like Plato’s Allegory of the Cave, the shadows dance upon the walls, and to the watcher are real together. In Morrison’s Beloved, the shadows depict the experience, the human suffering, trauma, and grief, and how they are all real, all painted on the walls. She shows how Beloved’s corporeal haunting makes it such that trauma is not either physical or an abstract, black or white, but even the abstract walks, talks, and demands recognition. In doing so, Morrison forces the reader to encounter how the corporeal and the physical body’s interpretations and manifestations are not separate, not mutually exclusive, but rather coextensive.

With her grand sweep of the human experience, Morrison uses Beloved as a vessel to examine the human comprehension of life, of the weight that it carries. Her overwhelming figurative language, her waves of imagery, and her complex literary contrast pave the way for a nuanced and fascinating understanding of how the real and the unreal can work together to achieve comprehension, how the psyche and the physical have to coexist for humanity to even come close to comprehending what it is that they have been through.

We now have the Categorical Imperative from Immanuel Kant. Kant is the preeminent Enlightenment philosopher who changed the entire playing field of ethics. Where do you get the rules from? - Logic, according to Kant. "Logic = Ethics" and "Ethics = Logic."

Logic is valuable for the use of ethics because logic does not change. Formal logic is unchanging: Categorical Logic, Propositional Logic, and Predicative Logic.

Immanuel Kant wants to show how formal logic can be used to determine the ethical validity of value statements. The difference between "Should" and "Have to" is in the results.

A Hypothetical Imperative is a hypothetical demand. A Categorical Imperative is a categorical (absolute, always true) demand. All imperatives command either hypothetically or categorically. Both are forms of logic, but Categorical Imperatives are unchanging and universally applicable.

Hypothetical Imperatives tell you the steps needed to achieve a goal in a specific situation. Logic is then locked into a very specific way to achieve that goal. Since Hypothetical Imperatives guide you towards a particular goal, they also tell you what not to do.

The Categorical Imperative, by principle, is the method by which we get the rules. Because logic is the means to good in the hypothetical, it can be argued that it is good. Logic, I mean. Logic defines good or bad action.

The Categorical Imperative is also the method by which we determine the rules.

Act only in accordance with that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it become a universal law.

The Categorical Imperative is a logic test:

  1. Make an action.
  2. Make it a moral rule.
  3. Apply that rule to everyone all at once.
  4. Look for contradictions.
